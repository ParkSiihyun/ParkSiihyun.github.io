Most M&A write-ups stop too early.

They summarize the buyer, the target, the deal value, and maybe the premium.
That is useful, but it does not answer the real question: **was this a good deal?**

To answer that, I need a repeatable framework. The goal is not to sound like a
banker. The goal is to understand why the transaction happened, what the buyer
believed, what price it paid for that belief, and what could go wrong.

This is the framework I will use for future deal case studies on this site.

## 1. Start with the transaction snapshot

Before forming an opinion, get the basic facts right.

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Who is buying whom? | Defines the strategic lens |
| What is the deal value? | Sets the scale of the decision |
| Is it cash, stock, or mixed consideration? | Shows risk sharing between buyer and seller |
| What premium is being paid? | Indicates how much control value is embedded |
| Is the deal completed, pending, or terminated? | Changes how much hindsight is available |
| What approvals are required? | Highlights regulatory and execution risk |

This section should be boring on purpose. If the facts are messy, the analysis
will be worse.

## 2. Write the one-sentence thesis

Every deal needs a one-sentence explanation.

> Buyer acquired Target because [strategic reason], and paid [valuation view] to gain [specific asset, capability, market position, or financial benefit].

If I cannot write that sentence clearly, I probably do not understand the deal
yet.

Examples of possible theses:

- A software company buys a workflow tool to deepen enterprise distribution.
- A bank buys a regional competitor to gain deposits and branch density.
- A pharmaceutical company buys a biotech asset to replace future patent cliffs.
- A consumer company buys a fast-growing brand because organic growth has slowed.

The thesis should be specific enough that it can be tested later.

## 3. Separate buyer logic from seller logic

A good deal analysis needs both sides.

### Buyer logic

The buyer is usually trying to solve one of these problems:

- Accelerate growth
- Enter a new market
- Acquire technology or talent
- Improve margins through scale
- Defend against a competitor
- Replace declining revenue
- Deploy excess cash

The key question is: **why buy instead of build?**

If the buyer could build the same capability cheaply and quickly, the acquisition
premium is harder to justify. If the target owns scarce distribution, data,
technology, regulatory access, or customer relationships, the acquisition may make
more sense.

### Seller logic

The target also needs a reason to sell.

Common explanations:

- The offer price is too attractive to reject.
- Growth is slowing and public market patience is fading.
- The company needs capital or distribution.
- Competitive pressure is increasing.
- Founders or investors want liquidity.
- The buyer can unlock value that the target cannot capture alone.

When the seller logic is weak, the process deserves more attention. Was there an
auction? Were there other bidders? Was management under pressure?

## 4. Understand the industry context

No deal happens in isolation.

I want to know what was happening around the transaction:

- Was the industry consolidating?
- Were financing markets open or tight?
- Were valuations high or low?
- Were regulators becoming more aggressive?
- Were competitors making similar moves?
- Was the target gaining or losing share?

This is where a deal becomes more than a press release. The same valuation can be
reasonable in one market cycle and reckless in another.

## 5. Build the valuation bridge

The valuation section should explain the path from standalone value to deal value.

At minimum, I want:

| Metric | What it tells me |
| --- | --- |
| Unaffected share price | Market view before the deal |
| Offer price | What the buyer agreed to pay |
| Premium to unaffected price | Control value and competitive tension |
| Enterprise value | Total operating asset value |
| EV / Revenue | Useful for high-growth or low-profit companies |
| EV / EBITDA | Useful for mature operating businesses |
| P / E | Useful when earnings are stable |
| DCF range | Intrinsic value under explicit assumptions |
| Comparable company multiples | Public market reference point |
| Precedent transaction multiples | M&A market reference point |

The most important question is not "what multiple did they pay?"

The better question is:

> What did the buyer need to believe for this price to make sense?

That belief might be higher growth, margin expansion, cost synergies, revenue
synergies, lower risk, or strategic scarcity.

## 6. Test the synergy story

Synergies are where many acquisition stories become too optimistic.

There are two broad types:

| Type | Usually easier or harder? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost synergies | Easier | They come from reducing overlap, consolidating systems, or improving purchasing power |
| Revenue synergies | Harder | They depend on customers changing behavior, sales teams executing, and products integrating well |

When analyzing synergies, I want to ask:

- Are the synergy targets quantified?
- How long will they take?
- What integration costs are required?
- Are they realistic compared with the size of the target?
- Do they justify the premium?

If management says the deal is about revenue synergies, I should be more
skeptical and more specific.

## 7. Look at financing and consideration

The form of payment matters.

### Cash deal

A cash deal gives the seller certainty. The buyer takes more of the future risk.
I want to know whether the buyer is using cash on hand, new debt, or both.

### Stock deal

A stock deal shares future upside and downside. It can also signal that the buyer
believes its own stock is attractively valued, or at least useful as acquisition
currency.

### Mixed consideration

A mixed deal balances certainty with participation. It can help close valuation
gaps when buyer and seller disagree on future prospects.

Financing also affects credit ratings, leverage, interest expense, and future
strategic flexibility.

## 8. Read the key deal terms

Deal terms show how risk is allocated.

Important terms include:

- No-shop: limits the target's ability to solicit other bids.
- Fiduciary out: allows the board to consider a superior proposal.
- Termination fee: compensates the buyer if the target walks away under specified conditions.
- Reverse termination fee: compensates the seller if the buyer cannot close.
- Matching rights: gives the buyer a chance to match a superior offer.
- Material adverse effect clause: defines extreme changes that may allow termination.
- Regulatory covenants: show how much effort the buyer must make to get approval.

The headline price gets attention, but the merger agreement often reveals the
real bargaining power.

## 9. Identify the main risks

I like to split risk into five buckets.

| Risk | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Valuation risk | Did the buyer overpay? |
| Integration risk | Can the companies actually combine operations, systems, and cultures? |
| Strategic risk | Does the thesis depend on assumptions that may not happen? |
| Regulatory risk | Could antitrust or other approvals block or change the deal? |
| Financing risk | Does the transaction weaken the buyer's balance sheet? |

The best case studies do not just list risks. They explain which risk matters
most and why.

## 10. Check market reaction

Market reaction is not always right, but it is useful evidence.

I want to check:

- Buyer stock price on announcement day
- Target stock price relative to offer price
- Credit spread or rating agency commentary
- Analyst notes
- Management's explanation on the deal call

If the buyer's stock falls sharply, the market may think the price is too high,
the strategic fit is weak, or the financing is unattractive. If the buyer's stock
rises, the market may see the deal as strategically or financially compelling.

## 11. Add post-deal read-through

For older deals, hindsight is an advantage.

I want to ask:

- Did the buyer keep or change the target's brand?
- Did the target grow faster after the deal?
- Were synergy targets reached?
- Did key employees stay?
- Did the acquisition change the buyer's competitive position?
- Would the same deal still make sense today?

This is where the analysis becomes judgment, not just summary.

## 12. End with a clear view

Every case study should end with an opinion.

My final section should answer:

- Was the strategic rationale strong?
- Was the price justified?
- Was the structure appropriate?
- What was the biggest risk?
- Would I have advised the buyer to do the deal?
- What is the lesson for future transactions?

The goal is not to be perfectly right. The goal is to make the thinking explicit
enough that I can improve it over time.

## The reusable checklist

For every M&A case study, I will try to answer these questions:

1. What happened?
2. Why did the buyer want the target?
3. Why did the seller agree?
4. Why now?
5. What price was paid?
6. What assumptions made the price reasonable?
7. How was the deal financed?
8. What terms protected each side?
9. What were the main risks?
10. How did the market react?
11. What happened after closing?
12. Was it a good deal?

That is the standard. Future case studies should make this framework more useful,
not just add more words.
